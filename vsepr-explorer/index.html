Two, three, and four electron groups

Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion theory predicts the 3D shape of a molecule from one idea: electron groups around a central atom push each other as far apart as possible. Drag any model to rotate it, and use the menu above each model to switch between example molecules.

Predicting a shape

  1. Draw the Lewis structure and identify the central atom.
  2. Count the electron groups (the steric number) on the central atom: each bonded atom counts as one, and each lone pair counts as one.
  3. Use that count to find the electron geometry (2 = linear, 3 = trigonal planar, 4 = tetrahedral).
  4. Note how many groups are lone pairs. The molecular geometry describes only where the atoms are, so it is named by ignoring the lone pairs.
  5. Adjust the angles: lone pairs squeeze bond angles below the ideal values.

What counts as one group

  • A single, double, or triple bond: one group each. Multiple bonds do not add groups.
  • A lone pair on the central atom: one group.
  • Lone pairs on terminal atoms do not affect the shape and are not drawn here.

AXE notation: A = central atom, X = bonded atoms, E = lone pairs. AX2E2 means 2 atoms and 2 lone pairs on the central atom.

Why angles shrink

Lone pairs are held by only one nucleus, so they spread out and take more room than bonding pairs. Repulsion strength:

lone pair–lone pair>lone pair–bond pair>bond pair–bond pair

Multiple bonds also repel a bit more than single bonds (H2CO: H–C–H is 116.5°, not 120°).

Polarity from shape

  • A molecule is polar when its bond dipoles do not cancel, giving a net dipole moment.
  • Symmetric shapes (linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral) are nonpolar if all terminal atoms are identical.
  • Shapes with lone pairs that break the symmetry (bent, trigonal pyramidal) are polar.
  • Mixing different terminal atoms (CH2Cl2, HCN) makes a symmetric shape polar.

Notes

  1. Measured angles are typical experimental (mostly gas-phase) values. Different textbooks may list values that differ by a degree or so.
  2. Octet rule. Central atoms from period 2 (C, N, O) never have more than 4 electron groups. Be and B are the common exceptions with fewer than 4 (BeCl2, BF3). Molecules with 5 or 6 electron groups exist for larger central atoms, but they are beyond this course.
  3. Resonance. In ions and molecules with resonance (CO32−, NO3−, SO2, O3…), the models draw a solid bond plus a faint second bond to represent the averaged bond order. Resonance never changes the number of electron groups.
  4. Models. Bond lengths are scaled from sums of covalent radii, shortened for double and triple bonds, so relative sizes are realistic but approximate. Atom colors follow the standard CPK convention. Lone pairs (purple lobes) are drawn on the central atom only.
